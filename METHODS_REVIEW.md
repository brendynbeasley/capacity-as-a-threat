# Methods review: Spence (1977) and Dixit (1980)

**Paper.** A. Michael Spence, "Entry, Capacity, Investment and Oligopolistic Pricing," *Bell Journal of Economics*
8(2), Autumn 1977, pp. 534–544 (DOI 10.2307/3003302). Read with its companion and correction, Avinash Dixit, "The Role
of Investment in Entry-Deterrence," *Economic Journal* 90(357), March 1980, pp. 95–106 (DOI 10.2307/2231658).

**Statistical focus.** The two formal claims that carry the pair: Spence's claim that entry-deterring capacity need
not be fully utilized before entry, so that deterrence produces idle capacity, higher costs and higher prices than
limit-price models imply; and Dixit's claim that, under linear demand and costs, an incumbent does not gain from
expanding output after entry, so capacity held purely as a threat is not credible.

**Review standard.** Internal consistency of the formal results, explicitness of assumptions, and the testability of
the predictions. For the empirical work later used to test them, standard reporting of estimates, uncertainty and
sample construction.

**What was read.** The full texts of both papers are restricted to JSTOR subscribers and were not available to me. I
read the bibliographic records for both (Crossref and IDEAS/RePEc), Spence's published abstract (IDEAS/RePEc), and
the abstract IDEAS attaches to Dixit's article, which it notes was borrowed from another version of the item. I read in
full Marvin B. Lieberman, "Excess Capacity as a Barrier to Entry: An Empirical Appraisal," *Journal of Industrial
Economics* 35(4), 1987, pp. 607–627, from the copy posted on the author's UCLA Anderson faculty page; it summarizes
both papers (pp. 608–609) and is the main empirical test of them. Statements below about the content of the 1977 and
1980 papers are limited to the abstracts and to Lieberman's summary. I did not read Bulow, Geanakoplos and Klemperer
(1985) beyond Lieberman's description of it.

## Methods summary

**Research design.** Both papers are deductive economic theory. Spence models established firms choosing capacity
before a potential entrant decides whether to enter, with capacity serving as an irreversible, pre-emptive commitment
(abstract). Dixit, per his abstract, studies one established firm facing one prospective entrant, where the
established firm's pre-entry decisions shape the entrant's view of what will happen after entry. Neither paper
collects data.

**Sampling strategy, sample size, inclusion and exclusion criteria.** Not applicable. No sample is drawn.

**Independent and dependent variables.** In the theory, the choice variable is the incumbent's pre-entry capacity;
the outcomes are whether entry occurs, the incumbent's output and capacity utilization before and after entry, and
the resulting price. The primitives are demand, the cost of capacity and of output, and the entrant's fixed cost.

**Statistical methods.** None. The "statistics" of these papers are their formal results.

**Reported effect sizes, confidence intervals, p-values.** None. The abstracts state directions (higher prices, lower
output, costs larger than necessary) without magnitudes. I could not check whether the full texts contain numerical
examples.

**Missing data treatment.** Not applicable.

**Stated limitations.** Not extractable from the abstracts. Lieberman (p. 609) lists the theoretical qualifications
raised afterwards: Dixit's linear-demand result, Bulow, Geanakoplos and Klemperer's counterexample under other demand
assumptions, free-rider problems among several incumbents, market growth and depreciation eroding the threat.

## Interpretation notes

- The two papers are not contradictory models of the same equilibrium so much as different credibility assumptions.
  Spence's entrant takes the incumbent's capacity as the post-entry output it will face; Dixit requires post-entry
  output to be the incumbent's best response after entry. The empirical content of the disagreement is whether
  deterring capacity sits idle before entry.
- Spence's prediction is about firms, while the most common public data on utilization are industry aggregates. A
  test at the industry level, like the one in this repository, can reject the claim that strategic idle capacity is
  widespread in concentrated, capital-heavy industries; it cannot rule out individual cases.
- Lieberman's design compares the utilization thresholds at which entrants and incumbents build. Equal thresholds
  are what non-strategic investment predicts, so his null result is evidence against routine pre-emption, as he
  says, but it is a test of a sufficient condition and could miss deterrence that operates through announcements or
  post-entry expansion, which he discusses separately (pp. 615–616, 625).
- Lieberman's significance tests are one-tailed for the main coefficients (Table IV note), which should be kept in
  mind when reading his reported significance levels.

## Unanswered questions

- Whether the full text of Spence (1977) contains a numerical example or parameterization, and what demand and cost
  assumptions it uses; this review could not check.
- Whether Dixit's no-idle-capacity result in the published paper is stated for linear demand only or more generally;
  I rely on Lieberman's one-sentence summary.
- How either model extends to several incumbents, which Lieberman notes raises free-rider problems.
- How large the price and cost effects of strategic capacity would be in a realistic calibration.

The modern rerun in this repository does not reproduce either paper, since neither has data. It tests the theory's
sharpest prediction, that idle capacity should concentrate where deterrence pays and go with fewer entrants, on Census
utilization and plant-opening data for 2008 to 2024, and runs Lieberman's incumbent-against-entrant comparison on the
Business Dynamics Statistics.
